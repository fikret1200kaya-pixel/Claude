# Video 4 — Costco: The Store That Doesn't Want to Profit From Products

**Title options:** How Costco Really Makes Money | Why Costco Won't Raise the $1.50 Hot Dog
**Target length:** ~7 min (~900 words)

---

## HOOK
There's a hot dog and a soda at Costco that has cost one dollar and fifty cents since 1985. Not through inflation. Not through recessions. Not through the pandemic. The CEO once told his successor: "If you raise the price of the effing hot dog, I will kill you." So how does a company make money by refusing to make money? Let's find out.

## ACT 1 — THE ORIGIN
Costco was founded in 1983 by Jim Sinegal and Jeff Brotman in Seattle, inspired by the warehouse club model pioneered by Sol Price at Price Club. Costco later merged with Price Club in 1993.

The idea is simple. A big, plain warehouse. Fewer products. Bulk packages. Low overhead. And customers must pay an annual fee just to walk in.

## ACT 2 — THE 14% RULE
Most supermarkets mark up products by 25 to 50 percent, sometimes more. Costco has an internal policy: it generally caps markups on branded goods at around fourteen percent, and its own Kirkland Signature brand at around fifteen percent.

That's incredibly thin. If Costco were a normal retailer, it would be struggling. Instead, it's one of the best-performing retailers in the world.

## ACT 3 — THE REAL BUSINESS
The secret: Costco's profit doesn't come mainly from products. It comes from memberships.

Membership fees bring in roughly four to five billion dollars a year, a big chunk of the company's operating profit. And because people have already paid to be there, they shop there, a lot.

Renewal rates are around ninety percent in the US and Canada. That's the real product: a loyal customer who comes back every year.

## ACT 4 — THE FLYWHEEL
Here's how it works. Low margins mean low prices. Low prices attract more members. More members mean bigger purchasing power. Bigger purchasing power gets better deals from suppliers. That allows even lower prices. And the cycle repeats.

Add the Kirkland Signature brand, which offers quality products at lower cost, and the loyalty grows even stronger.

## ACT 5 — THE PEOPLE
Costco also pays employees better than many competitors, with higher average wages and generous benefits. The company argues that happy employees mean lower turnover, better service and more efficient stores.

Wall Street has sometimes complained that Costco could make more by charging more or paying less. The company's leaders have consistently said the long-term trust of customers and employees is worth more.

## ACT 6 — THE LIMITS
It's not perfect. The model relies on huge volume and customer loyalty. The company has to keep proving that the membership is worth it. In 2024, Costco raised its membership fees for the first time in several years, a move customers and investors watched closely.

## ACT 7 — THE LESSONS
**One: Know where your real profit comes from.** Costco sells groceries, but its real product is trust and membership.

**Two: Consistency builds loyalty.** The hot dog price isn't about the hot dog. It's a promise.

**Three: Low margins can be strategy, not weakness.** When you design your whole business around them, they become a moat.

## OUTRO
Costco shows that the best business model isn't always the one that squeezes every dollar. Sometimes it's the one that earns your trust first. Next: a startup valued at forty-seven billion dollars that turned out to be built on very thin air.
