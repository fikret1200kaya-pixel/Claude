# Video 3 — Nokia: From 40% of the World to Zero

**Title options:** How Nokia Lost the Phone Market | Nokia's Fall: What Really Happened
**Target length:** ~7 min (~900 words)

---

## HOOK
In 2007, almost every second phone in the world was a Nokia. Same year, a man walked onto a stage in San Francisco and said: "Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone." Six years later, Nokia sold its phone business for a fraction of what it was once worth. How does the king of mobile lose its crown?

## ACT 1 — THE KING
Nokia started as a Finnish paper mill in 1865. Over the decades it moved into rubber, cables, and electronics, and in the 1990s bet everything on mobile phones.

It paid off. In the early 2000s Nokia was the world's biggest phone maker, with global market share around forty percent. Its phones were tough, simple, and sold everywhere. The company was so big it accounted for a noticeable share of Finland's entire economy.

## ACT 2 — THE IPHONE MOMENT
In 2007, Apple launched the iPhone. Nokia's initial response was not panic. Its phones were still selling in huge numbers, and its Symbian software was running on hundreds of millions of devices.

But the game had changed. A phone was no longer a device for calls and texts; it was a pocket computer. What mattered was the software, the apps, and the ecosystem. In 2008, Google's Android arrived too.

## ACT 3 — THE INTERNAL PROBLEM
Nokia's problems were as much about the inside as the outside.

Symbian was aging and hard to develop for. Different teams worked on competing software projects. Reports and later interviews with former managers describe a culture of fear, where middle managers hesitated to give bad news to top leadership, and a company so used to winning that it struggled to see a real threat.

Meanwhile, developers were choosing iOS and Android. Apps followed. Customers followed apps.

## ACT 4 — THE BURNING PLATFORM
In February 2011, Nokia's new CEO Stephen Elop, a former Microsoft executive, sent an internal memo that was quickly leaked. He compared Nokia's situation to a man standing on a burning oil platform, forced to jump into icy water. He said competitors had taken the lead and Nokia had fallen behind.

Nokia announced it would partner with Microsoft and use Windows Phone as its main smartphone platform. It was a dramatic bet. Many analysts argue it also hurt Nokia in the short term, because customers stopped buying old Symbian phones while the new Windows phones struggled to find an audience.

In 2013, Microsoft agreed to buy Nokia's devices and services business for about seven billion dollars. Within a few years, Microsoft shut down most of it and wrote off the investment.

## ACT 5 — THE SURPRISE ENDING
Here's the twist: Nokia the company survived. After selling its phone business, it focused on network equipment, acquiring Alcatel-Lucent in 2016, and today it builds telecom infrastructure used around the world. The Nokia brand name on phones is now licensed to another company.

## ACT 6 — THE LESSONS
**One: Market leadership can make you blind.** When you're winning, it's hard to believe the rules have changed.

**Two: In technology, the platform beats the product.** Nokia made great hardware. Apple and Google built ecosystems.

**Three: Culture decides speed.** If bad news can't reach the top, the company can't change course in time.

## OUTRO
Nokia's story isn't about one bad decision. It's about many small delays at the moment when speed mattered most. Next time: the store that deliberately doesn't want to make money on what it sells.
