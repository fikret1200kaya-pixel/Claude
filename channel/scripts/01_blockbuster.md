# Video 1 — Blockbuster: The $50 Million Mistake

**Title options:** How Blockbuster Turned Down Netflix and Lost Everything | The $50 Million Mistake That Killed Blockbuster
**Target length:** ~7 min (~950 words)

---

## HOOK
In the year 2000, a small startup walked into Blockbuster's headquarters with an offer. Buy us for fifty million dollars. Blockbuster's executives listened… and said no. Ten years later, that startup was worth billions. And Blockbuster? Bankrupt. This is the story of the most expensive "no" in business history.

## ACT 1 — THE EMPIRE
Blockbuster began in 1985 in Dallas, Texas. The idea was simple: big, bright stores, thousands of movies, and a guarantee you'd find what you wanted on a Friday night.

It worked. At its peak, in the mid-2000s, Blockbuster operated roughly nine thousand stores worldwide and employed tens of thousands of people. Friday night at Blockbuster was a ritual for a whole generation.

But there was a hidden problem. A huge slice of the company's profit didn't come from renting movies. It came from late fees. Every forgotten tape, every overdue DVD, quietly fed the machine.

## ACT 2 — THE OFFER
Meanwhile, in 1997, Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph had started Netflix. Their model: rent DVDs online and deliver them by mail. No store. No late fees.

By 2000, Netflix was losing money and needed a lifeline. Hastings and his team met with Blockbuster's leadership and proposed a deal: Blockbuster would buy Netflix for about fifty million dollars, and Netflix would run Blockbuster's online business.

Blockbuster passed. According to accounts from people in the room, the idea wasn't taken seriously. Online rental looked like a niche. Blockbuster had thousands of stores. Why bother with a tiny company shipping discs in red envelopes?

## ACT 3 — THE WAKE-UP CALL
Netflix kept growing. By 2004, it had millions of subscribers. Blockbuster finally woke up and launched its own online service and a program called Total Access.

It was aggressive. It was good. And in 2004, the company also took a bold step: it scrapped late fees in its stores.

But there was a catch. The change cut into revenue that Wall Street had come to depend on, and the company was also carrying heavy debt. Investor Carl Icahn pushed back hard. CEO John Antioco, who had championed the new strategy, clashed with the board over how much to spend, and in 2007 he left.

His replacement pulled back on the online push and went back to focusing on stores. By then, Netflix had already started streaming movies over the internet.

## ACT 4 — THE COLLAPSE
The timing was brutal. The financial crisis hit. Stores were closing. Redbox kiosks were renting movies for a dollar. And Netflix was now in living rooms, on game consoles, and on phones.

In September 2010, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy. In 2011, Dish Network bought its remaining assets for roughly three hundred million dollars.

Today, only one Blockbuster store remains open, in Bend, Oregon, and it has become a tourist attraction.

## ACT 5 — THE LESSONS
So what actually went wrong? Three things.

**One: They defended the old model instead of building the new one.** Late fees and stores felt safe, so the company protected them while customers moved on.

**Two: They mistook a small competitor for a harmless one.** Netflix looked tiny. But it was growing exactly where customers were heading.

**Three: They changed too late and without full commitment.** When Blockbuster finally moved, leadership fought itself, and the strategy was never given a real chance.

Netflix, on the other hand, made a habit of cannibalizing itself. It moved from DVDs to streaming, then from streaming to making its own shows. It chose to disrupt itself before someone else did.

## OUTRO
Blockbuster didn't die because it was stupid. It died because it was comfortable. The question every business has to ask is simple: are we protecting what made us successful, or building what comes next?
