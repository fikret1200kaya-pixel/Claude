# Video 2 — Kodak: Invented the Digital Camera, Then Went Bankrupt

**Title options:** Kodak Invented the Digital Camera… Then Died | How Kodak Killed Itself
**Target length:** ~7 min (~900 words)

---

## HOOK
1975. A young engineer at Kodak builds a strange gadget: a toaster-sized box with a lens, a tape recorder, and a tiny screen. It's the world's first digital camera. His bosses look at it and say: "That's cute, but don't tell anyone about it." Thirty-seven years later, Kodak files for bankruptcy.

## ACT 1 — THE GIANT
George Eastman founded Kodak in the late 1800s with a simple slogan: "You press the button, we do the rest." Photography became something for everyone.

For about a century, Kodak dominated. In the United States it controlled the vast majority of film and camera sales. At its peak in the 1980s it employed well over a hundred thousand people. Kodak made money the way a razor company does: sell the camera cheaply, then earn forever on the film.

## ACT 2 — THE INVENTION
In 1975, Steve Sasson, a Kodak engineer, built the first self-contained digital camera. It captured a 0.01-megapixel black-and-white image and took about twenty-three seconds to record it onto a cassette tape.

Sasson later said management's reaction was less excitement and more confusion: Why would anyone want to look at pictures on a TV screen? And, more importantly, it threatened Kodak's most profitable business: film.

Kodak did patent the technology and even sold early digital cameras in the 1990s. But the leadership kept protecting film, which was still printing money.

## ACT 3 — THE TRAP
This is the classic innovator's dilemma. Digital photography would be cheaper, but it would also destroy the film revenue. Every step toward digital felt like cutting off the branch the company was sitting on.

So Kodak hesitated. Competitors didn't. Japanese companies like Sony, Canon and Fujifilm pushed digital hard. Fujifilm, Kodak's rival in film, used its chemistry know-how to expand into other industries such as healthcare and cosmetics. Kodak stayed too attached to photos and film.

Then came phones. By the 2000s, cameras were built into mobile phones, and sharing photos online replaced printing them.

## ACT 4 — THE COLLAPSE
Kodak's film sales fell sharply year after year. In January 2012, Kodak filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection.

The cruelest comparison came that same year. Facebook bought Instagram, a photo-sharing app with just thirteen employees, for about one billion dollars. Kodak, the company that once defined photography, had once employed over a hundred thousand people.

Kodak emerged from bankruptcy in 2013 as a much smaller company focused on commercial printing and imaging, and sold off many of its patents.

## ACT 5 — THE LESSONS
**One: Having the invention isn't enough.** Kodak had the technology first. What mattered was whether it was willing to commit.

**Two: Protecting your cash cow can be fatal.** If you don't cannibalize your own product, someone else will.

**Three: Know what business you're in.** Kodak thought it was in the film business. It was really in the business of capturing and sharing memories. Smartphones and social media did that better.

## OUTRO
Kodak didn't miss the future. It saw it, built it, and then decided it wasn't ready. In business, sometimes the biggest risk is doing nothing.
