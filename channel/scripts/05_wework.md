# Video 5 — WeWork: The $47 Billion Illusion

**Title options:** How WeWork Fooled Everyone (For a While) | WeWork's $47 Billion Collapse
**Target length:** ~8 min (~1000 words)

---

## HOOK
In January 2019, WeWork was valued at forty-seven billion dollars. That's more than Ford. More than Twitter. Less than a year later, the company's IPO was cancelled, its founder was out, and its value had fallen off a cliff. What was WeWork really? A tech company? A real estate firm? Or something else?

## ACT 1 — THE DREAM
WeWork was founded in 2010 by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey in New York. The idea: rent offices on long leases, split them into flexible desks and offices, add beer, coffee, design and community, and rent them out to freelancers and startups on short terms.

It looked cool. It felt like the future of work. And early on, it grew fast.

## ACT 2 — THE STORY
Neumann was a charismatic storyteller. He didn't pitch WeWork as real estate. He pitched it as a "community company," a movement, even a "world-changing" consciousness company.

That framing mattered. Real estate companies get modest valuations. Tech companies get huge ones. Investors, led by SoftBank's Masayoshi Son, poured in billions. WeWork's valuation climbed to forty-seven billion dollars in early 2019.

## ACT 3 — THE PROBLEM
Underneath, the economics were hard. WeWork signed long-term leases, often ten to fifteen years, then rented space short-term. In good times, that works. In bad times, it's dangerous: the rent still has to be paid when customers leave.

The company was also losing huge amounts of money while spending aggressively to grow.

## ACT 4 — THE IPO
In August 2019, WeWork filed documents to go public. For the first time, the public could see the numbers.

They showed big losses, roughly two billion dollars in 2018 alone, along with massive lease commitments. They also showed unusual governance: Neumann had super-voting control, and had been paid for the use of the "We" trademark, which the company agreed to buy from him, a deal that drew heavy criticism. Reports of several related-party deals raised more concerns.

Investors pushed back. The valuation talk fell from tens of billions to a fraction of that. In late September 2019, the IPO was shelved, and Neumann stepped down as CEO.

## ACT 5 — THE AFTERMATH
SoftBank stepped in with a rescue package worth billions, and Neumann left with a huge payout reported at well over a billion dollars. WeWork then struggled through the pandemic, when remote work cut the demand for offices.

It went public through a SPAC merger in 2021, but the business never reached steady profit. In November 2023, WeWork filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection, and emerged from it in 2024.

## ACT 6 — THE LESSONS
**One: A story is not a business model.** Narratives can raise money. Only unit economics can sustain a company.

**Two: Growth without profit is a bet, not a strategy.** It works only while investors keep believing.

**Three: Governance matters.** When one person has too much control and too few checks, mistakes become disasters.

**Four: Match your costs to your revenue.** Long-term obligations funded by short-term income is a fragile structure.

## OUTRO
WeWork's idea wasn't useless. Flexible workspace is still a real market. What failed was the gap between the story investors were sold and the business that existed. Subscribe for more stories of how companies are built… and how they come apart.
