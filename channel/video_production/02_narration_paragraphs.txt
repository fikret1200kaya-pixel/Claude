Nineteen seventy-five. A young engineer at Kodak builds a strange gadget: a toaster-sized box with a lens, a tape recorder, and a tiny screen. It's the world's first digital camera. His bosses look at it and say: "That's cute, but don't tell anyone about it." Thirty-seven years later, Kodak files for bankruptcy.

George Eastman founded Kodak in the late eighteen hundreds with a simple slogan: "You press the button, we do the rest." Photography became something for everyone.

For about a century, Kodak dominated. In the United States it controlled the vast majority of film and camera sales. At its peak in the nineteen eighties it employed well over a hundred thousand people. Kodak made money the way a razor company does: sell the camera cheaply, then earn forever on the film.

In nineteen seventy-five, Steve Sasson, a Kodak engineer, built the first self-contained digital camera. It captured a zero point zero one megapixel black-and-white image, and took about twenty-three seconds to record it onto a cassette tape.

Sasson later recalled that management's reaction was less excitement and more confusion. Why would anyone want to look at pictures on a TV screen? And, more importantly, it threatened Kodak's most profitable business: film.

Kodak did patent the technology, and even sold early digital cameras in the nineteen nineties. But the leadership kept protecting film, which was still printing money.

This is the classic innovator's dilemma. Digital photography would be cheaper, but it would also destroy the film revenue. Every step toward digital felt like cutting off the branch the company was sitting on.

So Kodak hesitated. Competitors didn't. Japanese companies like Sony, Canon and Fujifilm pushed digital hard. Fujifilm, Kodak's rival in film, used its chemistry know-how to expand into other industries, such as healthcare and cosmetics. Kodak stayed too attached to photos and film.

Then came phones. By the two-thousands, cameras were built into mobile phones, and sharing photos online replaced printing them.

Kodak's film sales fell sharply, year after year. In January two thousand twelve, Kodak filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy protection.

The cruelest comparison came that same year. Facebook bought Instagram, a photo-sharing app with just thirteen employees, for about one billion dollars. Kodak, the company that once defined photography, had once employed over a hundred thousand people.

Kodak emerged from bankruptcy in two thousand thirteen as a much smaller company, focused on commercial printing and imaging, and sold off many of its patents.

So what are the lessons?

One. Having the invention isn't enough. Kodak had the technology first. What mattered was whether it was willing to commit.

Two. Protecting your cash cow can be fatal. If you don't cannibalize your own product, someone else will.

Three. Know what business you're in. Kodak thought it was in the film business. It was really in the business of capturing and sharing memories. Smartphones and social media did that better.

Kodak didn't miss the future. It saw it, built it... and then decided it wasn't ready. In business, sometimes the biggest risk is doing nothing.
